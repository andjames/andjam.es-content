What happened in Nepal was terrible. It's one of the most beautiful places on Earth — and one of the friendliest. It's heavily touristed because of those reasons. Unfortunately, it's also incredibly prone to glacial lake outbursts and flooding in narrow valleys.

I was in Nepal in 2003 as part of a project with <a href="https://portersprogressuk.org/about/">Porter’s Progress</a> to assess and install first-aid kits for porters working along the trail to – and at – Everest Base Camp.

Even then, locals talked about the rising water at Imja Tsho, the glacial lake below Imja Tse (Island Peak). There were dark jokes by locals about how one day a flood would wipe out everything in the valley below.

I hiked past Imja Tse on that trip. I had a restless night sleeping near the growing lake after hearing about it.

In 2010, I went back for more selfish reasons: to climb Imja Tse.

It rises like an island out of the middle of the valley and it’s beautiful. From the summit, you look down about 1,000 metres to Imja Tsho. It's a long (strangely swimming pool-shaped) rectangle at the end of a retreating glacier.

It’s actually in the background of my Gmail profile photo from the summit. And it's visibly grown since then.

I’m writing this because Imja Tsho has the potential to devastate communities downstream. The systems in place right now for understanding and communicating early warnings aren't good enough – especially compared to what is available. Online monitoring data is pretty fragmented.

What's frustrating is that this is a problem we can actually solve pretty simply with open data.

There is tons of Earth-observation expertise. We have satellites with eyes always on the Himalayas. We have cheap sensors, robust forecasting systems and communications infrastructure that wasn't available in 2003 – and had just started to show up in the valley in 2010.

Each year, people spend $50,000 — sometimes up to hundreds of thousands of dollars — just to stand in a long line on Everest.

There must be some combination of Earth-observation community, tech cos that crosses over with climbers, expedition operators, NGOs and people with money to burn can build and maintain an actual functioning system to monitor glacial lakes – warn families downstream when conditions change.

Imja Tsho is not alone. Other glacial lakes across the Himalayas are at dangerous thresholds. And Nepal isn’t alone either.

We don't need to solve the entire problem before doing something useful.

Start with one lake as a proof point. 

The infrastructure costs are a fraction of an Everest permit costs.

Make the data reliable. Make it open and public. Build out satellite observations with instruments on the ground (NASA just hosted an EarthData webinar on streaming data). 

This needs a redundant alerts that actually reach people downstream. Maintain it. Publish the system so it can be replicated elsewhere.

We can start with Imja Tsho, but we need to start now.
